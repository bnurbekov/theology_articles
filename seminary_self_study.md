# A Self-Study Path Toward Reformed Seminary Knowledge

*How to approximate the readable content of a Reformed (1689 Baptist) seminary education on your own — an ordered reading list with reasoning, a free-of-charge tier built from public-domain works and free online courses, honest notes on what reading cannot deliver, and how to get those missing parts.*

---

## Before you begin: what this can and cannot do

A self-study reading plan can genuinely approximate the *knowledge* content of a Reformed seminary — systematic theology, biblical theology, church history, hermeneutics, and confessional study are all learnable from books, often the very books seminaries assign. What it cannot deliver are the three things a seminary is uniquely built around:

1. **The biblical languages**, drilled to a usable level (calendar-gated by spaced repetition, not compressible by daily hours).
2. **Mentored pastoral formation** — preaching with feedback, supervised ministry, pastoral care.
3. **Examination, accountability, and credentialing** toward ordination.

So this article covers the reading fully, and then turns honestly to those three. For anyone pursuing pastoral ministry, the reading is the *easy* part; the load-bearing step is a conversation with the elders of a church who would form and eventually examine or recommend you. Their pathway, not any reading list, determines what you actually need.

Where these recommendations come from: the titles are drawn from actual Reformed seminary syllabi (RTS assigns Bavinck and Calvin in systematics; PRTS and CBTS structure their programs around the same divisions used below), from standard-textbook status across evangelical schools (Carson & Moo, Dillard & Longman), and from confessional fit for the 1689 Baptist tradition (Waldron). It is deliberately single-tradition — a real syllabus assigns opposing views too, which this does not, a limitation worth supplementing where you can.

## Part 1 — The reading list, in order

The order moves from *how to read Scripture* → *the content of Scripture* → *systematic synthesis* → *church history* → *defense and application* → *the confession*. Each stage prepares the next.

### Stage 1 — Foundations: how to read Scripture
1. **J. Scott Duvall & J. Daniel Hays, *Grasping God's Word*** — a widely assigned hermeneutics textbook. Read first so everything downstream is read with method rather than instinct.
2. **Geerhardus Vos, *Biblical Theology*** — the foundational Reformed work tracing how revelation unfolds across redemptive history. Dense but foundational; the lens Reformed preaching depends on.

### Stage 2 — The content of Scripture (survey and background)
3. **Raymond Dillard & Tremper Longman III, *An Introduction to the Old Testament*** — book-by-book coverage: authorship, date, structure, themes.
4. **D. A. Carson & Douglas Moo, *An Introduction to the New Testament*** — the NT counterpart, one of the most widely used in evangelical seminaries.

### Stage 3 — Systematic theology (the synthesis)
5. **Louis Berkhof, *Systematic Theology*** — a clear, compact distillation of the Reformed system. Read before Calvin so you meet the whole framework first.
6. **John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*** — the fountainhead of Reformed theology; far more navigable with Berkhof's system already in hand.
   *(Optional deepening: Herman Bavinck, *Reformed Dogmatics*, one-volume abridgment — what RTS actually assigns.)*

### Stage 4 — Church history
7. **Bruce Shelley, *Church History in Plain Language*** — the standard readable one-volume survey; placed after systematics so the doctrinal controversies land with weight.

### Stage 5 — Defense and application
8. **John Frame, *Apologetics to the Glory of God*** — the accessible entry to the presuppositional (Reformed) approach. *(Classical apologetics — e.g. Sproul — is the Reformed alternative worth knowing.)*
9. **John Frame, *The Doctrine of the Christian Life*** — Reformed ethics; placed late because it applies everything prior.

### Stage 6 — Pastoral craft and the confession
10. **D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones, *Preaching and Preachers*** — the reading side of homiletics. (The skill itself comes only from preaching with feedback.)
11. **Samuel Waldron, *A Modern Exposition of the 1689 Baptist Confession*** — read last, bringing everything to bear on your confessional standard.

**Reading time:** roughly 6,750 pages. At 4 hrs/day (~40 pp) that's ~6–8 months; at 6 hrs/day (~60 pp) ~4 months. The dense titles (Calvin, Vos, Carson & Moo, Frame's ethics) run slower than the page count suggests — treat these as a floor.

## Part 2 — The free-of-charge tier

Counting free online courses and lectures alongside public-domain books, roughly **90% of this curriculum can be assembled for free and legally.** Here is what substitutes cleanly, what half-substitutes, and where a modern purchase (or free course) genuinely earns its place.

### Fully covered by free classics
- **Systematics:** Berkhof (~$1 or free-legal), plus **Charles Hodge, *Systematic Theology*** (3 vols, public domain, free at CCEL and Internet Archive) and **Calvin's *Institutes*** (free at CCEL/Monergism). Compact, deep, and primary-source systematics at no cost.
- **Church history:** **Philip Schaff, *History of the Christian Church*** (8 vols, free at CCEL) in place of the modern Shelley — longer, but complete and free.
- **Ethics:** **Thomas Watson, *The Ten Commandments*** (free at CCEL) for the classical core — moral law, the Decalogue, conscience, the Christian life. Supplemented by the ethics sections within Hodge/Berkhof and the Puritan casuistry tradition (**William Ames, *The Marrow of Theology***). It does not cover *contemporary applied* ethics — add a modern source only if a specific present-day question requires it.
- **Preaching/pastoral:** **Richard Baxter, *The Reformed Pastor*** and **C. H. Spurgeon, *Lectures to My Students*** (both free at CCEL/Monergism) stand in fully for the reading side of Lloyd-Jones.
- **Confession:** the **1689 text** itself is public domain (free at reformedreader.org and church sites).
- **Biblical theology:** **Vos** — effectively free/public-domain depending on edition.

### Fully covered by free online lectures (not books)
- **Presuppositional apologetics:** the method is 20th-century (not public-domain as books), but its core teaching was released free as *lectures*:
  - **Greg Bahnsen, *Van Tillian Apologetics*** (4-part series, free on Westminster Seminary's site) — the best starting point.
  - **Bahnsen's full ~30-lecture seminary course** — free as MP3s.
  - **Cornelius Van Til's** own lectures and writings — free at vantil.info and Presuppositionalism 101.
  - Curated collections at Monergism and The Domain for Truth aggregate dozens of series. These are *core* teaching by the method's own systematizer, not a compromise.

### Mostly covered by free online courses
- **OT/NT introductions:** free seminary-level survey courses at **BiblicalTraining.org** — **Bill Mounce's *Survey of the New Testament*** (his master's-level seminary class) and **Douglas Stuart's *Old Testament Survey***. Plus **BibleProject Classroom**. These cover most of the survey content; they are lighter on the deep *critical-introduction* layer (detailed authorship/dating debates), so add **Dillard & Longman** / **Carson & Moo** (or their cheaper abridgments, ~$10 and ~$17) only if you will be examined on that layer.

### Half-covered (free option is older/weaker)
- **Hermeneutics:** **Louis Berkhof, *Principles of Biblical Interpretation*** or **Milton Terry, *Biblical Hermeneutics*** (both public domain) — usable but older and less friendly than the modern Duvall & Hays.

### The honest ledger
| Category | Free substitute | What you give up |
|---|---|---|
| Systematics | Berkhof + Hodge + Calvin | Nothing meaningful |
| Church history | Schaff (8 vols) | Length/readability |
| Ethics (classical) | Watson, *Ten Commandments* | Contemporary application only |
| Preaching | Baxter + Spurgeon | Nothing meaningful |
| Confession text | 1689 (free) | Waldron's modern exposition |
| Biblical theology | Vos | Nothing — same book |
| Apologetics | Free Bahnsen/Van Til lectures | Nothing on the method itself |
| OT/NT intros | Free courses (Mounce, Stuart) | Deep critical-introduction layer only |
| Hermeneutics | Berkhof / Terry | Modern pedagogy and clarity |

**Where to download (all free, all legitimate):** CCEL (ccel.org), Monergism (monergism.com), Internet Archive (archive.org), Grace-eBooks, reformedreader.org. **Free courses/lectures:** BiblicalTraining.org, BibleProject Classroom, Westminster Seminary media, vantil.info.

## Part 3 — Biblical languages

Languages are a separate track, not another book on the pile. Two honest points: the binding constraint is *calendar time, not daily hours* — vocabulary sticks through spaced repetition over months, so ~1.5–2 focused hours a day is the productive ceiling per language. And the realistic target is **working knowledge** (enough grammar and high-frequency vocabulary to use the tools well and follow an exegetical argument), not full fluency.

The winning method for this goal: learn the grammar *as a system* first (a few months of real work), then read the biblical text daily with a tool that lets you *check your own attempts* — not one that simply hands you the answer. The reading is where vocabulary sticks; the grammar foundation is what turns reading into learning rather than guided guessing.

- **Standard textbooks:** **William Mounce, *Basics of Biblical Greek*** and **Pratico & Van Pelt, *Basics of Biblical Hebrew*** (each with a workbook). Used copies are inexpensive.
- **Free resources:** Mounce's free chapter lectures (billmounce.com, BiblicalTraining.org); **Daily Dose of Greek / Daily Dose of Hebrew** (short daily videos); **Aleph with Beth** (inductive Hebrew) and **Alpha with Angela** (Greek). For reading the text with word-level support: **STEP Bible** (Tyndale House), **Blue Letter Bible**, **Bible Hub**.

**Timeline:** roughly 6–9 months to working Greek, 9–12 to working Hebrew, best done sequentially — about 15–20 months for both, running in parallel with everything else.

*Why the languages, if you have lexicons?* A lexicon gives a *range* of meanings; choosing the right one requires reading the grammar and syntax, which is exactly what a lexicon cannot do for you. Grammar and syntax carry meaning (a genitive that is subjective vs. objective changes a doctrine); and the contested passages where you'd most want the original are exactly where surface-level tool use goes wrong. Working knowledge is the level at which the tools *help* you rather than mislead you.

## Part 4 — What reading cannot give you, and how to get it

Comparing this plan against an actual Reformed Baptist M.Div. (CBTS: exegetical theology 33 credits, systematic 23, practical 19, historical 12) surfaces three gaps.

**1. Original-language exegesis.** Two layers: (a) the languages themselves, self-teachable with external structure to survive the attrition that stalls most solo learners; and (b) the *exegesis skill* on top — reasoning from the original text to a sound conclusion, which needs correction from someone who knows the languages. Layer (b) overlaps with mentoring below.

**2. Mentored pastoral formation.** The one part that cannot be self-supplied. Arrange it deliberately: approach the elders of a church you respect and ask for a formation relationship — regular meetings, opportunities to preach or teach with feedback, gradually increasing responsibility under oversight. This is the historic Reformed Baptist way of training men. For counseling specifically, the IRBC certification (reformedcounseling.org) is available on its own. This single relationship supplies exegesis correction, formation, and accountability at once.

**3. Examination, accountability, credentialing.** Accountability and breadth can come from mentoring (a pastor assigning reading outside your tradition, examining you, pushing back). A *credential*, if your path requires it, comes only from an institution or an ordaining body's examination — and whether you need it depends entirely on the church or association that would ordain you. Some ordain by examination (no degree required); others require the degree. Confirm this before deciding anything else.

**Self-assembly vs. a program.** Everything above can be assembled piecemeal, or a program matched to this exact situation can package it. **Covenant Baptist Theological Seminary** is 1689-subscribing, built for bivocational distance students who stay in their own church, and integrates all three gaps — original-language exegesis, a pastoral-mentoring program tied to your own church's elders, IRBC counseling certification, and accredited examination — at $100/credit, or tuition-free through a partner church, requiring a pastoral recommendation to enroll. Self-assembly is far cheaper (roughly $150–$400 for books and languages) and saves time *on the reading*; but the languages (~15–20 months) and formation (~6–12+ months) run on the same clock either way, and self-assembly carries a higher risk of stalling. Which is worth it turns on whether the credential matters for your ordination path and whether you will actually finish unstructured.

## The one thing to do first

Whether you self-assemble or enrol, both routes start in the same place: **a direct conversation with the elders of a church you would seek to serve and be ordained through.** Ask plainly: *how would you want me to prepare for and enter pastoral ministry, and does our path to ordination require a degree or an examination?* Their answer determines which gaps you must close formally, whether you need a credential, and whether the languages are required. That single conversation is worth more than any further reading or planning — because for pastoral ministry, the path runs *through* a congregation that recognizes and confirms a calling, which no reading plan alone can supply.
