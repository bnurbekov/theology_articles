Theological Disciplines: A Two-Track Reading List

A concise, self-directed theological curriculum following the general structure of A. A. Hodge’s Outlines of Theology, with additional disciplines for conservative Reformed Baptist ministry.

How to use this list

Track A — Free and public-domain resources

Prioritize complete, legally accessible texts. Each resource should be classified as:

* PD: Public domain in the United States; verify the specific edition.
* FREE: Full text accessible at no cost, but redistribution rights are not established.
* REFERENCE: Primarily a reference work rather than a first textbook.

Track B — Modern recommendations

Copyrighted textbooks recommended when their added value justifies the cost. Prefer one primary textbook per discipline; treat other works as supplementary.

Three stages of study

1. Foundations: biblical interpretation, biblical theology, introductory systematic theology, the 1689 Confession, and introductory biblical languages.
2. Historical and doctrinal depth: church history, historical theology, ethics, apologetics, and Particular Baptist covenant theology.
3. Ministry preparation: preaching, church polity, pastoral counseling, evangelism, missions, and supervised ministry.

These stages overlap. Begin practical ministry under appropriate church oversight while continuing academic study.

Overview of the disciplines

* Track A — PD: A. A. Hodge, Outlines of Theology (1878); Abraham Kuyper, Encyclopedia of Sacred Theology (1898).
* Track A — FREE: A. A. Hodge’s Outlines of Theology and other historical works can be located through Internet Archive and Princeton Theological Seminary’s digital collections.
* Track B: No additional introductory textbook required.

Use Hodge and Kuyper for orientation, not as substitutes for the actual study of the individual disciplines.

Apologetics

* Track A — PD: Joseph Butler, The Analogy of Religion (Project Gutenberg).
* Track B: Cornelius Van Til, The Defense of the Faith.

Butler is a historical classic; Van Til offers a distinctly Reformed approach to apologetics. Read the latter if affordable rather than treating Butler as a comprehensive modern apologetics course.

Exercise: Write a short defense of the Christian faith and explain how you would respond to one major objection.

Biblical languages

Greek grammar

* Track A — REFERENCE: A. T. Robertson, A Grammar of the Greek New Testament in the Light of Historical Research (1914; Internet Archive search).
* Track B: William D. Mounce, Basics of Biblical Greek; Daniel B. Wallace, Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics.

Hebrew grammar

* Track A — REFERENCE: Gesenius, Hebrew Grammar, translated and edited by Kautzsch and Cowley (Wikisource).
* Track B: Gary D. Pratico and Miles V. Van Pelt, Basics of Biblical Hebrew Grammar.

Method: Begin with a beginner’s textbook, learn vocabulary and paradigms, translate actual biblical passages, and only then use advanced grammars for difficult constructions.

General introduction and hermeneutics

* Track A — PD: Milton S. Terry, Biblical Hermeneutics (Internet Archive search).
* Track A — PD: Frederick Scrivener, A Plain Introduction to the Criticism of the New Testament (historical reference on textual criticism; Internet Archive search).
* Track B: No additional textbook required initially.

Exercise: Interpret a passage by identifying its literary context, historical setting, structure, argument, and relationship to the rest of Scripture before consulting commentaries.

Special introduction

* Track A — PD: B. F. Westcott, An Introduction to the Study of the Gospels; C. F. Keil, Manual of Historico-Critical Introduction to the Canonical Scriptures of the Old Testament.
* Track B — New Testament: D. A. Carson and Douglas J. Moo, An Introduction to the New Testament.
* Track B — Old Testament: Edward J. Young, An Introduction to the Old Testament.

Study authorship, date, historical setting, literary genre, canonical status, and theological purpose. Do not assume that a nineteenth-century introduction represents every aspect of current scholarship.

Exegesis and commentaries

* Track A — PD: John Calvin, Commentaries (CCEL).
* Track B: Choose a reliable modern commentary for the biblical book being studied rather than purchasing a complete commentary series immediately.

Method: First produce your own translation or close reading, outline the passage, identify the main argument, and explain its theological significance. Consult commentaries afterward to test and refine your conclusions.

Biblical theology

* Track A — PD: Gustave Friedrich Oehler, Theology of the Old Testament (Internet Archive search).
* Track A — PD: Bernhard Weiss, Biblical Theology of the New Testament (Internet Archive search).
* Track B: Geerhardus Vos, Biblical Theology: Old and New Testaments.

Vos is the preferred core work for understanding the progressive unfolding of revelation in Reformed biblical theology. Oehler and Weiss are historical supplements, not exact substitutes for Vos.

Exercise: Trace one theme—such as covenant, kingdom, temple, sacrifice, or priesthood—from its early biblical foundations through its development and fulfillment in Christ.

Biblical theology of the church

* Track A — PD: James Bannerman, The Church of Christ (Internet Archive, Volume I; Volume II search).
* Primary biblical texts: Genesis 12 and 17; Exodus 19; Matthew 16 and 28; Acts 2 and 15; Ephesians 2 and 5; 1 Timothy 3; Revelation 21–22.
* Confessional supplement: Second London Baptist Confession of Faith (1689), chapter 26.

Bannerman is Presbyterian, not Particular Baptist. Use him comparatively and evaluate his arguments on church membership, covenant continuity, ordinances, and government against Scripture and Baptist convictions.

This section should trace the development of God’s covenant people, the kingdom, temple, gathering of the nations, and the church’s consummation—not merely list doctrines about the church.

Systematic theology

* Track A — PD: Charles Hodge, Systematic Theology (three volumes; CCEL, Volume I).
* Track A — PD: John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion (CCEL).
* Track B: Louis Berkhof, Systematic Theology.

Use Berkhof as the primary systematic framework if accessible. Supplement it with the 1689 Confession and specifically Particular Baptist treatments of covenant theology, baptism, and ecclesiology. Do not assume that every Reformed Presbyterian conclusion transfers unchanged to a Baptist framework.

Exercise: Write a concise doctrinal statement on one subject, citing the relevant biblical texts and comparing your conclusion with the 1689 Confession.

Ethics

* Track A — PD: Archibald Alexander, Outlines of Moral Science; Joseph Butler, Dissertation on the Nature of Virtue.
* Track B: John Murray, Principles of Conduct; John Frame, The Doctrine of the Christian Life.

Study the relationship between biblical commands, Christian liberty, conscience, holiness, and the application of moral principles to concrete situations.

Historical theology

General church history

* Track A — PD: Philip Schaff, History of the Christian Church; William G. T. Shedd, History of Christian Doctrine; Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom.
* Track B: No additional general survey required initially.

Schaff is valuable but extensive. Begin with a manageable survey or selected periods before attempting the entire series. Read older historians with awareness of their theological and historiographical assumptions.

Reformation and Particular Baptist history

* Track A — PD: Study the primary historical documents of the English Reformation, the Particular Baptists, and the development of the Second London Baptist Confession (1689).
* Track A — PD: Nehemiah Coxe, A Discourse of the Covenants That God Made with Men Before the Law (Internet Archive search).
* Track B: No additional textbook required initially.

Give particular attention to the development of Particular Baptist covenant theology, confessional subscription, baptism, church membership, and congregational government.

Practical theology

Homiletics

* Track A — PD: John A. Broadus, On the Preparation and Delivery of Sermons; Richard Baxter, The Reformed Pastor (Internet Archive search).
* Track B: John Stott, Between Two Worlds; Haddon Robinson, Biblical Preaching.

Exercise: Prepare and deliver an expository sermon. Submit the manuscript or outline to a qualified pastor for criticism and revision.

Church polity

* Track A — PD: John L. Dagg, A Treatise on Church Order (Internet Archive search).
* Track A — PD: Benjamin Keach, The Glory of a True Church, and Its Discipline Display’d (Internet Archive search).
* Primary confessional source: Second London Baptist Confession (1689), chapter 26.

Dagg is the principal reading. Keach provides a historical Particular Baptist supplement. Study the biblical basis of church membership, elders, congregational authority, discipline, ordinances, and church autonomy.

Exercise: Write a short position paper on the authority and responsibilities of elders and the congregation, defending each claim from Scripture.

Pastoral counseling

* Track A — PD: Richard Baxter, A Christian Directory (Project Gutenberg, Part I; Part II; Part III; Part IV).
* Track B: No additional textbook required initially.

Baxter provides a substantial historical resource for practical Christian living, spiritual struggles, family responsibilities, and cases of conscience. It is not a complete modern counseling manual.

Study how to counsel with Scripture, listen carefully, identify the nature of a problem, maintain confidentiality appropriately, recognize limits of pastoral competence, and refer people for professional medical or mental-health care when needed.

Exercise: Work through hypothetical pastoral cases and explain your proposed counsel, biblical rationale, and any need for outside assistance.

Evangelism and missions

* Track A — PD: William Carey, An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens (Project Gutenberg).
* Track A — PD: Andrew Fuller, The Gospel Worthy of All Acceptation (Internet Archive search).
* Track B: No additional textbook required initially.

Carey addresses the church’s missionary responsibility; Fuller addresses the duty of sinners to believe the gospel and the theological basis for earnest evangelistic invitations.

Distinguish the theological basis of evangelism, practical gospel communication, church planting, and cross-cultural missions.

Exercise: Prepare a clear gospel presentation, practice explaining it to another person, and develop a simple plan for evangelistic outreach under appropriate church oversight.

Study method and assessment

For every major discipline:

1. Read the principal work selectively or completely according to its purpose.
2. Write a short summary of the author’s main argument.
3. Identify the biblical evidence and the principal objections.
4. Compare the conclusions with the 1689 Confession where relevant.
5. Produce a written exercise or practical demonstration.
6. Seek feedback from a knowledgeable pastor, elder, or teacher.

Notes on access and rights

* PD means public domain in the United States only after checking the particular edition and its publication history.
* A free download does not automatically grant permission to redistribute or host the text.
* Archive and library search links are discovery links; confirm that the edition is complete and legally accessible.
* Modern introductions, editorial notes, translations, and reprints can have separate copyright protection.

Remaining limitations

This list is a self-study bibliography, not an accredited seminary program. It does not replace supervised original-language instruction, pastoral mentoring, regular church service, preaching evaluation, or the examination and accountability of a local church.

The objective is not to finish the greatest number of books. It is to grow in biblical understanding, doctrinal accuracy, historical awareness, and practical fitness for faithful ministry.

Final assessment

The links are not all equivalent:

* Confirmed free full texts: Butler, Carey, Baxter’s four parts, Calvin’s commentaries, the 1689 Confession, and Schaff’s works.
* Identifiable works, but access uncertain or restricted: Berkhof, Vos, Bannerman, Robertson, Shedd, Fuller, and several other historical works.
* Modern textbooks: the publisher links identify the books but do not establish free access.
* Incomplete or weak links: Hodge's online text is incomplete; Gesenius's transcription is marked incomplete; several Archive.org links are searches rather than direct book pages.
